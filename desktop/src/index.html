Plug in your Canary

Connect it over USB-C with a data cable. It's picked up automatically — the status bar above tracks it live, no button to press.

🛡 New board from a shop or marketplace? Do this first — hold BOOT while plugging in

It arrives running somebody else's firmware. On a board with native USB (the S3 and C-series Canaries) that firmware can introduce itself to your computer as anything it likes, including a keyboard, the moment you plug it in. No app can step in front of that; your operating system finishes with the device before this one even hears about it.

One move stops it, and it's the chip's own silicon doing the stopping:

  1. Unplug the board, if it's plugged in.
  2. Press and hold BOOT (marked B).
  3. Still holding it, plug the cable in. Then let go.

No BOOT button, or no USB port at all? A classic ESP32 — an ESP32-CAM, a plain WROOM DevKit — reaches your computer through a separate USB-to-serial chip rather than its own USB, so its firmware can only ever appear as a serial port and cannot pretend to be a keyboard. Bringing it up cold is still how you stop the resident firmware running: jumper IO0 to GND before you apply power, instead of holding BOOT. Most DevKits do even that for you. Per-board wiring is on the board's own card below.

The chip comes up in its mask-ROM download mode and the firmware it shipped with never runs a single instruction. That's not a promise this app is making — it's unerasable ROM, the same property that makes the board impossible to brick from here.

The browser flasher can also read this chip's security fuses to tell you whether a previous owner locked it. The espflash tool this app drives has no fuse-read command, so that one check is browser-only for now — it isn't being run and reported as clean here.

Found, but not identified?

Put it in download mode: hold BOOT (B), tap RESET (R), then let go of BOOT — it'll be read automatically. Leave download mode later with a tap of RESET.

    Advanced — you can skip all of this
    

            
    
              The automatic safety copy
              
    Before every write, the whole chip is read to a timestamped file in this
                app's backups folder — the undo button. Restore any copy from Rescue below.
                Skipping saves ~a minute per board on a big batch; the board you're
                erasing anyway loses nothing, but a provisioned Canary loses its undo.
    
              
            
    

            
    
              Something's wrong — copy a diagnostic report
              
    One click gathers what a bug report needs — app build, platform, chip,
                USB device, chosen firmware, the last error, and the console tail —
                and puts it on your clipboard. Nothing is sent anywhere; you decide
                where it goes.
    
              
    
                
                
              
    
              
              
            
    

            
    
              Dev channel — flash the rolling prerelease
              
    Dev images come from the rolling fw-dev-latest
                prerelease — cut ahead of stable, verified exactly the same way (chip
                guard, SHA-256, and the release signature once the signing ceremony
                lands). The toggle can only ever mean fw-dev-latest;
                it is not a way to point at an arbitrary manifest.
    
              
            
    

            
    
              Install a local file
              
    Install a factory firmware image from your computer
                (a factory .bin you built — dev_flash.sh <env> -f
                or firmware/scripts/make_factory.py makes one —
                or one saved for an air-gapped setup). An app-only build won’t do: it
                gets written at offset 0, where only a merged factory image belongs.
                We can’t check a personal file’s signature, but the board still can’t
                be bricked, and we verify the write against the chip.

              Health check — read the board’s story (changes nothing)
              
    Read the board’s own record straight off flash — the partition
                map, the firmware in each slot and when it was built, update
                history, whether it ever hard-crashed, and its witness chain —
                and get a plain-language verdict with what to do about anything
                off. Read-only: nothing is written to the board,
                and the identity key and saved Wi-Fi are never shown or saved —
                only whether they’re present is reported.
    
              
    
                
              
    
              
    
              
    
              
    
              
    
            
    

            
    
              Rescue bench — back up, restore, or erase a board
              
    Turn the flasher into a safety net: pull a full copy of the chip
                to a file you keep, write a saved copy (or any factory
                .bin) back, or wipe the chip to a clean
                slate. A backup holds everything on the board —
                its Ed25519 identity key and saved Wi-Fi included — so store the
                file like a house key. None of this can brick the board: the
                ESP32’s first-stage bootloader is mask ROM.

              Skip Home Assistant's setup wizard — pre-make your account
              
    Type your login now and this app finishes it for you: keep the app
                open through first boot, and the moment the hub answers, it creates your account on
                the hub over Home Assistant's own setup API and checks the login works —
                so your first visit is a sign-in, not a wizard. Your password stays on this computer
                (a scrambled copy also rides the card as a head start) and goes only to your own hub,
                on your own network.
    
              
    
                
                
                
                
              
    
              
    
            
    

            
    
              Let this app finish hub setup by itself — broker, MQTT, securaCV
                experimental
              
    The card also carries the securaCV self-setup bundle and a
                maintenance key made on this computer. Once the hub is online, this app
                connects to it over your network and installs everything — the Mosquitto broker, the MQTT
                connection, Frigate, and the securaCV witness kernel — narrating why at every step. The Pi
                never needs a monitor or keyboard; this screen is the screen.
    
              
    The key's private half stays in this app's data folder and never leaves this
                computer. To revoke it later, delete authorized_keys from the
                card's boot partition (or re-flash without this option).
    
              
              
    Recommended companion — Pi-hole.
                A small open-source DNS service millions of people run. Your devices ask it
                “where is this website?”, it answers, and it refuses known ad and tracker domains
                along the way. It's here because securaCV's promise is devices that
                don't talk out — and Pi-hole is how you check that promise instead of
                taking our word: one page shows every domain every device (Canaries included) tries
                to reach, so anything quietly phoning home shows up in plain sight. It changes
                nothing until you point your router's DNS at the hub, and skipping it changes
                nothing at all.
    
              
    What it records, plainly: to tell you which
                device asked for a domain, Pi-hole has to log the asking device — so its query log
                holds the client's IP address (and hostname, if your network supplies one), the
                domain, and the time. It does not see what's on a page, only the names looked up.
                That log is the feature, and it is also a log of your household's browsing: it stays
                on your hub, nothing is uploaded, and you control how long it's kept — Pi-hole's own
                settings let you shorten retention or turn query logging off entirely (blocking
                still works without it).
